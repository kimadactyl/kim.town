*This is a write-up of my presentation notes from the *[*9th Anarchist Studies Network conference*](https://anarchist-studies-network.org.uk/)* in Manchester, August 2026, titled: &#x201c;The old world is dying, the new world is struggling to be born: anarchist organising in an age of platform dependency&#x201d;.*

*The slides are available at *[*slides.gfsc.community/asn9*](https://slides.gfsc.community/asn9)*.*

## Part 1: The world we’re in

How many groups are you in that entirely depend on WhatsApp? What’s your work / activism / life balance like there? Have you ever tried to leave it? Are you in *any* organisation that manages without it?

The core problem I think everyone faces right now is that the signal to noise ratio for being involved in organising work is *dreadful*. Many of us are in dozens of groups pinging at all times of day with no way to distinguish idle chatter from crucial decisions.

This is the WhatsApp Activism Complex or WAACLEX (*please* let’s make this a thing). Our entire organising lives are mediated by billionaires who hate us and fund our enemies, and we need to stop.

If WhatsApp died tomorrow, it wouldn’t just be an inconvenience. &#x30de;&#x30ea;&#x30a6;&#x30b9;’s piece [&#x201c;The Day WhatsApp Goes Dark&#x201d;](https://xn--gckvb8fzb.com/the-day-whatsapp-goes-dark/) lays out the scale: WhatsApp has 3.3 billion monthly active users, and runs on 99% of smartphones in Brazil, with governments running public health services through it. As the piece puts it, WhatsApp for most practical purposes *is* the internet.

We used to own some of this stuff not so long ago. We had Indymedia and IMCs, and groups were run with email lists. Where is our daily attention going now? A lot of places but primarily Facebook, Instagram and WhatsApp: some of the most corporate and surveilled places on earth. Our platforms and identities are no longer portable: an email address or our own website is something we control ourselves, an Instagram handle is not. To make it worse, using these platforms makes our enemies rich by selling adverts and personal data, as I talked about in my previous piece [*Full Meta Jacket*](https://gfsc.community/full-meta-jacket-how-corporations-colonised-queer-life-and-what-we-can-do-about-it/)*.*

Many organisations are one shadowban, copyright strike or terms-of-service change from losing their entire audience. I feel like every few weeks I see another organisation pleading to help get a profile reinstated, and this will only get worse as government and corporate repression intensifies. This is hostile infrastructure working as designed aka ‘enshittification’, as corporations work to *reduce* the interoperability and openness of the web: for example, Facebook events don’t really work any more, RSS and iCal have been quietly deprecated, and algorithms and hyper-targeted ads mean we don’t even have the same experience of the same sites.

While online life was being captured, accelerated hugely by the ongoing Covid-19 pandemic, community venues have been dropping like flies. The death of third spaces and the privatisation and enclosure of online spaces feel like two sides of the same coin: to me you no longer need a physical space to feel politically actualised when you can just reshare infographics on Instagram. We have a generation radicalised online without the physical spaces to act out those politics in the real world. I think this is why collective action feels so hard right now.

## Part 2: You lot probably know all this already

A room full of anarchists doesn’t need telling that alternatives exist. Most of us are doing our best: the conference chat was on Signal (a non-profit), lots of people run WordPress and reach for open source when they can, and a few hands went up for recognition of [Mastodon](https://gfsc.community/community-spotlight-mastodon/) (even if people aren’t on it themselves yet).

But almost *all* the software we use is now rented from corporations, and open source culture can be shitty and often places the blame on individuals for this. We now have an all-new neoliberal choice capitalism, like lifestyle veganism or anti-flying politics, with all the guilt and shame that comes with it: if you’re using any big-tech software you’re a bad person, if you’re not on Linux you’re a bad person, if you’re on whatever default platform everyone uses you’re a bad person, if you touch AI at all you’re a bad person, and community venues that use AI to make posters apparently deserve to go out of business. I think this is a load of elitist crap and we are not getting anywhere until we develop compassion and understanding for each other doing our best to survive the Torment Nexus in real time.

&#x201c;Ethical tech consumption&#x201d; doesn’t work as a sole motivator. When Discord’s age-verification panic hit, searches for alternatives went up 10,000% overnight and Element claimed two million signups. But often the alternatives aren’t accessible, so there isn’t even an easy ethics here. And beyond that, what is the &#x201c;ethical&#x201d; amount of computing resource for us to have? I would argue there’s *never* been  time where computing has been ethically just. People go where their audience is and where they think they can best effect change. In Discord’s case, I think they were one of the first people to enact a law that’s out of their control. I just wish the anger was directed at the *laws themselves,* as any other platform that needed to replace them would end up with the same limitations once it reached that scale.

We need collective technology for collective organising. Anarchist pragmatism cuts both ways: we’re brilliant at making do, but over time it means we’re always on the back foot and never own our means of production.

## Part 3: How can we bridge the infrastructure gap?

&#x201c;Free as in beer&#x201d; will always beat &#x201c;free as in freedom&#x201d; when you’re skint. Most people want to be spending less time on the computer, not more. Let’s be honest: DIY tech for any single task is expensive, frustrating, time consuming, less reliable and buggier compared to the off-the-shelf thing that’s had a few million dollars of VC poured into it.

Free-as-in-beer is its own politics too, a dumpster dive mentality, using what corporate resources we can live in the cracks of, as we try to do whatever it is we came here to do. But the corporate ‘free’ in this case over time is death by a thousand cuts: surveillance, moderation policies written by your enemies, data centres run on poverty wages. Building alternatives is just a completely different conversation to surviving capitalism: it’s much harder, takes a lot longer, and requires us to cooperate in ways we’re not used to.

Working out how to bridge this gap is basically GFSC’s *entire project*, and it’s been really nice to re-situate this within an explicitly anarchist politics. To briefly summarise [our manifesto](https://gfsc.community/the-geeks-for-social-change-manifesto-v1-0/), here’s a rough sketch of how we think we get out this mess:

**Work with people and their communities, not ‘users’.** The way out isn’t (just) better software. People have had bad experiences with tech and with tech practitioners, and we need to bridge the gaps between &#x201c;tech&#x201d; and &#x201c;non-tech&#x201d; culture. We need broader conversations about what social processes we actually want to make easier: what do people want to **be** and **do**? (Nussbaum, 2011). Everyone is a tech person: we all live in the house technology built (Franklin, 1989).

**Start where we are right now.** Most orgs need help with immediate stuff: email, forms, websites. Most people need to use *less* things, not more. Supporting people with that builds trust. Reduce, reuse, recycle. What existing communities are we in, and how can we support them? And how do we get the resources to properly support the tech workers creating this infrastructure? *(i.e. us lol - speaking of which, join our email list and/or *[*support us*](https://gfsc.community/support-us/)* in other ways?)*

**Develop shared infrastructure.** We need our own collectively owned tech infrastructure in the same way we would share a building, a van, or a PA. Not every org needs to be running its own server: we can identify shared needs and create common resources. It’s cheaper than ever to have a VPS (Hetzner starts around 5 to 10 euros a month) and one server can run multiple apps. Can we site them in community centres and other places we already hold like the [Iwi Maori](https://www.kahuiraraunga.io/panui/world-first-data-storage-infrastructure-solution-built-by-iwi-maori-for-iwi-maori) have shown? We’re working on ways to do this right now, blog coming soon.

This is the work Geeks for Social Change exists to do: [PlaceCal](https://placecal.org/) aggregates thousands of events and activities from hundreds of local community organisations across the UK. These are lifted from our forthcoming Community Technology Canvas, a tool to map your community’s digital capability, which we will be releasing very soon. Some questions to ask to start a conversation about collective technology, both in person and digitally:

- **Gut feeling:** what’s the one thing you would like to fix right now?
- **People:** how do you join or leave your org?
- **Community:** what ways are members able to connect with each other?
- **Physical places:** how do these connect to physical spaces?
- **Digital places:** how do people map to user accounts, communities map to online spaces, and places map to websites and social media profiles?
- **Impact:** how do we measure if it works?
- **Economy:** how do we pay for it?
- What’s one thing that would make everyone’s life easier?
