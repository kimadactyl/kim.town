## The image of the sun

![Towards a Lunarpunk Practice, Part 1: The Sun](https://gfsc.community/content/images/2026/07/image-1785406946402.jpg)The power of empire is the power of the sun. The sun’s power is literally extraterrestrial, singular, distant, with everything oriented towards it and drawing power from it. Louis XIV, the Sun King, cultivated his image as the centre of the universe and arranged his court around himself, all power flowing inwards. Alice Sparkly Kat’s [*Postcolonial Astrology*](https://www.alicesparklykat.com/postcolonialastrology/), the inspiration for this piece, proclaims: “In a spectacular society, visibility does not emerge from existence but from the **orientation toward power**.”

Courting the power of empire means competing for ideas at the highest level, capturing control of the public discourse. Why do some ideas seem to matter more than others? Right now, the idea that seems to matter most is AI, or more specifically, like four very specific AI companies. It’s almost impossible to have a conversation anywhere about *anything* without it being a conversation about AI, love it or hate it. But there’s nothing natural about this: it’s a discourse dictated by the court (the tech billionaires), using their power (via the stock market) to make everyone else scrabble to get some scraps from their table (why not launch a startup?).

![Towards a Lunarpunk Practice, Part 1: The Sun](https://gfsc.community/content/images/2026/07/Ambassade_Perse_aupre--s_de_Louis_XIV.jpg)
*Louis, looking *literally* radiant (via [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_XIV#/media/File:Ambassade_Perse_aupr%C3%A8s_de_Louis_XIV.jpg))*

The apparatus of empire in the image of the sun is the stock market. A tiny minority of people on earth control the vast majority of company stock. Statements like [“Elon Musk is a trillionaire”](https://www.forbes.com/sites/pr/2026/06/12/forbes-declares-elon-musk-as-the-worlds-first-trillionaire/) only make sense in this logic-of-empire, where the discourse is controlled by how much ultra-rich people think other ultra-rich people are ‘worth’, based on who they think is making the most plausible financial bets about the future of society. There is no way Musk could ever actually withdraw and own that much money — I’ve seen estimates putting it in the hundreds of millions if he tried to withdraw it all. The world of the stock market is a world of nested IOUs between the ultra wealthy that lets them live outside the mainstream economy.

If there’s one thing the current wave of far-right autocrats has managed to do incredibly well, it’s to constantly keep themselves the centre of attention: constantly the thing that is debated, constantly positioned at the centre of the court as the obvious one to make decisions. This is why: patriarchal racist capital *needs* these ideas and people so it can continue to be the centre of attention. This is the logic of the big-tech-AI-sun-empire: keep everything oriented towards power, at all costs.

In the UK we have such a thing as a state pension, but in the USA (and anywhere with only private pensions), a pension is simply a long term investment in an index fund that tracks the stock market. **It is not just companies need AI to work, or the tech industry, or the stock market: everyone needs AI to work if they want to be able to *retire*.** Or at least, a better idea has to come along that can take its place. This is the real power of empire: even if you want nothing to do with the things it creates, your ability to survive depends on it. When our empire fails, we all lose.

## Towards a lunarpunk practice

This is a bit of an experimental piece, and I guess a chance to out myself as a bit of a novice astro girlie. I’m still quite new to these ways of thinking and understanding, but the symbolism of the sun, moon and planets from Sparkly Kat’s book has felt like a veil being lifted to help reveal deep-seated psycho-social trends that make the world tick along the way it does. Specifically in the world of technology it feels like there is a way of doing things *so* embedded it’s hard to point to: an orientation-towards-the-sun that ends up being reproduced even in the attempts to resist it.

To counter this, I’m experimenting with the idea of my personal practice and my work at GFSC being a *Lunarpunk* practice, in contrast to more established (but still culturally minoritised!) ideas of *Solarpunk*. Lunarpunk started life as a sci-fi aesthetic, the nocturnal sibling of solarpunk: night, mystery, bioluminescence, the occult. Solarpunk never quite hit home for me, feeling more like the updated version of the “let’s get all my mates and live on a commune in the country” fantasy that so often feels like white settler-colonialism in miniature, a *Settlers of Catan* LARP about escaping society to a mythical unspoiled isle (I’m aware this is probably unfair and I would love some reading suggestions for things that would confound that feeling!).

Lunarpunk feels like the perfect frame for me to explore my transfeminist personal politics and think about how tech can be used to *resist* by building the world *we* want to build, in defiance of empire but not defined by it, or unwittingly copying its logic. Resistance work has always been done in the shadows, at night, with cunning and guile, using the sun’s reflected light to create different worlds. That’s the practice I want to explore.

## The goose that lays the golden AI

Sparkly Kat’s book talks about gold as the representative of the sun. They cite Guy Debord, who compared the spectacle to “the sun which never sets over the empire of modern passivity”, covering the entire surface of the world and bathing “endlessly in its own glory”. Gold was extracted by empires and then put in vaults in financial centres like London and New York. Historically gold wasn’t a thing for individuals to spend, but rather for empires to own in order to prove their wealth and stature. Obviously it is traded now, but the way it’s moved around is virtual. The actual gold sits in vaults.

SoftBank is one of the biggest tech investors in the world (and were dramatised in the TV series [WeCrashed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WeCrashed) — deffo worth a watch). An investor presentation of theirs was [shared online last month](https://bsky.app/profile/edzitron.com/post/3mp3ij3fv3k2j) showing this insane logic. SoftBank show a golden goose laying golden eggs, in [a dystopian presentation](https://group.softbank/media/Project/sbg/sbg/pdf/ir/investors/shareholders/2026/shareholders-meeting_46_05_en.pdf) about removing humans and replacing them with the kind of warzone AI robots we’ve all seen enough sci-fi movies to know are *definitely* not the good guys, and an extreme level of control where all of our infrastructure is run by these companies.

![Towards a Lunarpunk Practice, Part 1: The Sun](https://gfsc.community/content/images/2026/07/image.png)
*Softbank says: in a disaster: these things are coming to get you.*

Through a series of actually insane pseudo-algebraic sums, they show how the golden goose not only lays golden eggs, but the golden eggs themselves will continue to grow forever. In fact, they have a golden egg machine. (One slide literally just reads “Eggs do not lay eggs”: only the goose, you see, creates value.) This is what Sparkly Kat calls the “self-breeding nature of money”, which “has continued to influence how we expect the financial sector to behave: as a thing that must constantly grow and produce”. All of this continues to valorise *ideas* and diminish *labour*: where the materials come from, who it’s built by, how it’s built, who it’s for, and so on, are all obfuscated.

What we have here is a sanitised, disenchanted world. Tielas’ quote again: “extraction is a relationship possible only in a world proclaimed *always already dead*”. Social relations, world resources and workers’ jobs are irrelevant: all you need to know, Mr Rich Person, is that we can deliver you golden eggs forever. Louis XIV’s court lives on.

## Ideas, resources, and the cloud

Every technology is both a system of ideas and a system of resources.

Perhaps the biggest thing Silicon Valley has done is make technology appear invisible. “The cloud” as a literal metaphor that encourages us to think of technology as immaterial, other, distant, intangible. The seamlessness with which you can now access a web app without needing to know a tech person or get your hands dirty yourself. You can log in in a browser and use millions of computers in countries you’ve never been to, on servers moderated by people you’ve never met, owned by companies you’ve never heard of. Somehow all of this feels permanent, trustworthy and reliable despite all evidence showing otherwise.

Let’s take Twitter as an example. I think Twitter popularised not just the idea of microblogging, but alongside early SaaS apps like Facebook and Gmail popularised this seamless, simple, easy ‘app-based’ interface with the world. Somehow Twitter’s version of microblogging has become a trend in itself, reproduced endlessly by a plethora of other platforms with similar affordances: a live timeline, everything fits in one screen, very short comments, emphasis on a single action (compare to Facebook’s bloat), algorithms to put viral content in front of you, and design affordances to encourage posting passing thoughts frequently.

What made Twitter work though was the software and the user base, but it was also the position it gained from VC investment and harnessing the power of the USA’s empire. For a while there *was* one site for a global public conversation, everyone orienting themselves around the same sun. The features are much less relevant than the fact your favourite celebrities and football stars and your local politician are on there, and that you can @ the council to moan about your bin collection and actually have them respond.

Musk, who has since bought Twitter, has made the platform technically much, much worse. But he doesn’t care, because this is all about keeping him at the centre of the discourse, regardless of how much it costs him. Now that the pattern is established, people want to reproduce it. So, in part because of Twitter’s enshittification, and in part because everyone wants to have their own sun, we now have Bluesky, Threads, Truth Social, W Social, and yes, Mastodon, making almost carbon copies of the interface and logic behind it.

## Why open-source alternatives feel disenchanted

I think open source alternatives are falling into the trap of competing for the sun’s logic. I wrote [recently about (temporarily) discontinuing our Mastodon server](https://gfsc.community/why-we-discontinued-our-mastodon-server/), and I don’t want to keep picking on this one project but it has been a rich seam to explore. To me, Mastodon and its user base is attempting to directly compete with other microblogging sites, frequently comparing itself to Bluesky in terms of features or protocol and wondering why more people don’t join it. I think this is missing a trick. Sun-empire sites work *precisely because* of the sun logic. Its power has to come from elsewhere.

There was a threat of a wide exodus from Discord earlier this year, when they announced mandatory age verification in line with a wave of new age-verification laws. [Searches for alternatives spiked 10,000% overnight](https://www.webpronews.com/the-great-discord-exodus-age-verification-backlash-sends-gamers-scrambling-to-alternatives-that-cant-keep-up/), [TeamSpeak’s servers were overwhelmed](https://kotaku.com/discord-alternative-teamspeak-age-verification-check-rivals-2000669693), and [Element claimed two million new signups](https://matrix.org/blog/2026/02/welcome-discord/) on the back of it. Signups are perhaps not the most useful metric though: were those accounts ever used again? How would we even know? Many people tried to propose alternatives, but they were just worse versions with fewer features and none of your friends were on them.

Fundamentally there was no reason for people to leave Discord *apart* from the ethical one, which, in my experience, is never a big enough argument by itself. It’s basically asking what’s more important: accessibility (both socially and in terms of corporate software having far better ‘a11y’), or some value of ethical consumption. I think people getting mad at the law change have the wrong target, too, because any service is going to have to comply with this eventually, once it reaches a sufficient scale. So where do we place the individual against the group here? It can’t just be about individually escaping.

We need to do this collectively.

## Lunarpunk re-enchantment

What could it look like to resist the sun’s logic and make technology in the image of the moon? Some very early, untested and slightly silly suggestions follow that build on our [philosophical foundations](https://gfsc.community/state-your-assumptions-gfscs-philosphical-foundations/). These are just early ideas for a lunarpunk manifesto, and I will develop these in future pieces.

### Explore spellwork

Perhaps one of the few pieces of software to get referred to not by its name is WordPress. Sun-logic platforms enforce the platform and the ‘thing’ being one and the same (you have “a Patreon” or “a Substack”). WordPress, on the other hand, has become a thing you don’t normally mention is WordPress any more. Once set up, it’s just&#x2026; the name of your website. When the blogosphere took off, it didn’t really seem to matter what platform you used, just as long as you had a blog. Corporate vendors rely on you saying their brand name and have done a lot to reverse this.

We can think of this as spellwork. The software should matter less than what the software is being used to do. So we need to cast spells: name things by their intention, not their function. For example, Mastodon is overwhelmingly called Mastodon, I rarely see people talking about the name of their server as the ‘thing’ they are on (e.g. Lurk, Hachyderm, Assemblages), and more’s the pity. I think until we’re talking about the name of the thing being more to do with the organisation that’s hosting it, and why they’re hosting it, we’re just reproducing sun logic.

### Make feminised labour and natural resources explicit

Work should be visible, supported, and celebrated. We should know where our servers are and who runs them, and celebrate and support that. Anyone who has tried to support their community with technology knows how utterly thankless it can be: an unpaid tech support who people only contact in a pissy mood when things break.

As discussed in our Mastodon piece though, moderation especially is in crisis stage with fewer people than ever wanting to help out with moderation. This places server owners on a social and legal knife-edge that feels like a disaster waiting to happen.

To re-enchant we must think about what it means to do this kind of labour, celebrate who does it, and think about how to integrate this work into the rest of our lives and communities. We need to talk about the mechanisms of the system as a route to our collective liberation.

The sun’s logic is clean, perfect, ephemeral, literally the image of a cloud with little angels sitting on it. As soon as we start to try and challenge this power, all of our hands end up getting dirty, whether we like it or not. We should celebrate and centre this work and make it easier for people to see the absurdity of the invisible hand of empire.

### Start a network of covens

The sun’s form relies on vertical integration. In a vertically integrated service, the brand, software, logo, logic, login, company and support service are all the same thing with the same name. Centralisation breeds simplicity, and a simplicity of understanding.

Trying to create a lunarpunk world necessarily requires disassembling this, and asking each question individually: how do we want our identity to be stored? What information do we want to share, and where? How we want to communicate? How do we want to relate to one another? What’s an appropriate amount of sharing? What should be foregrounded? Should the person who posts once a month get just as much attention as the person who posts a hundred times a day?

We need to talk about the problems we’re trying to solve, not just the platforms we’re trying to replace. These conversations can be very hard to have but happen through ongoing processes of trust and relationship building between groups of people (or covens) working in these areas.

### Cultivate both solemn secrets and bold proclamations

Modern social media has created a culture where it feels normal to share everything about us and our creative processes online. The obvious reaction to that has been a swing back to privacy and a desire to get offline.

I’d love to think of it instead as more of a consciously-decided spectrum of possibilities. What can we keep between ourselves and develop a sense of sacred friendship? What needs shouting to the world? When I produced the [Resistance Lab Taser Report](https://resistancelab.network/our-work/taser-report/index.html) this almost happened by accident – dozens of small group and private conversations across a year or so culminating in a razor sharp report we were then able to publish as a zine and distribute widely. What if we supported each other with this through our network of covens, and built the network infrastructure to do it?

### Celebrate different kinds of witchcraft

There are [dozens of types of witches](https://www.wikihow.com/Types-of-Witches) all practising and prioritising different aspects. These lists are enormously varied and show hugely different strategies and scales of effect, with the pluralism being a strength in itself. Unfortunately white Western left wing culture can be very insistent there is [one right way to do things](https://www.whitesupremacyculture.info/one-right-way.html). I’d suggest instead we could shift to trying to understand each other as practitioners of different arts, celebrate this plurality of strategy, and build a network we can call on when the time is right.

### Build a temple

What if we started treating our infrastructure like ancient libraries, arcane ruins, forbidden gems or sacred machinery? We are in a time of technological abundance but it’s not going to last forever. Given computers as we now know them are probably impossible to create without megacorporations, I think it’s probably a good time to imagine living in a time of scarcity and wondering what that would feel like.

[The Iwi Maori](https://waateanews.com/2024/06/04/world-first-data-storage-infrastructure-solution-built-by-iwi-maori-for-iwi-maori/) [have built their own datacentres](https://www.rnz.co.nz/news/te-ao-maori/596199/maori-owned-data-storage-network-hailed-as-significant-step-towards-data-sovereignty) situated in community buildings and I think this is a really really good idea: making the physicality of our computing resources visible and accessible like that is both great for pedagogy and sense of ownership. Keeping everything virtual and distant doesn’t allow us to think of these as real resources.

## A lost example

When I think about what it would look like to re-enchant our social media, I come back again to the Indymedia heyday: specifically the network of computers (thin client linux clusters that a lot of us got our first taste of open source in), computer suites (in the network of activist social centres there at the time) and pre-corporate-social-media local independent media collectives (when “be the media!” was the rallying cry) that existed up and down the country, using and maintaining the system together. The Indymedia I remember was built on Drupal, but you’d never know this unless you got pretty heavily involved in development.

This situating of groups, people and computers within existing activist and resistance infrastructure was pretty revolutionary in a way I didn’t realise until it was gone. Having this all in one place meant we had control of our own end-to-end process where we could support and train people up to write an article without having to know anything about the underlying stack or how the software worked. We no longer really do this kind of media work in public any more — corporate social media has taken over as I detailed in my [Full Meta Jacket](https://gfsc.community/full-meta-jacket-how-corporations-colonised-queer-life-and-what-we-can-do-about-it/) piece. I really think the loss of this kind of integrated, place-based information work has had tremendous negative effects on our ability to think of computing as a communal resource.

The enchantment happens when people are meeting in physical spaces, and when we’re setting an intention together to do something in the world. Technology is one often vital part of that intention, but the intention must come *first*. There’s no enchantment in thinking about features and software and adoption and active users, where everyone has to be an individual expert in all the technologies they’re using. Enchantment comes through community, and allowing people to be part of a supported whole: not individuals who have successfully isolated themselves from the internet everybody else uses.
