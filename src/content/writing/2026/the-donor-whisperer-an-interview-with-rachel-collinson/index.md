**Kim**
Hello everyone. I’m Dr Kim Foale, and this is the Geeks for Social Change podcast. It’s been a while since we’ve done one of these. We’re going to try a new format where we do some interviews with people we like. For our first one, I have with me a long-time Geeks for Social Change supporter and ally, all-around good egg, Rachel Collinson, aka&#xA0;[the Donor Whisperer](https://www.donorwhisperer.co.uk/). So welcome, Rae.

**Rae**
Oh, you flatter me. Thank you, I’m honoured to be here.

**Kim**
So tell us a little about you. How would you say you’ve come to be here?

**Rae**
I’m a consummate geek, really. My journey started when I was five years old. My uncle, bless his heart, bought me a Sinclair ZX Spectrum. I was really into robots and tech things, and I was delighted, and immediately started to learn how to code. It was quite difficult at five years old, because I could barely type. I did all the classic BASIC programming things you do at that age, but that started off a lifelong love of technology. I ended up studying graphic design, because I was obsessed with New Order and Peter Saville and their approach to album covers. And then when I got to art college, I realised graphic design wasn’t as exciting as I’d hoped. But there was a bit of the course where you could do multimedia, and I realised I could marry my love of computers with my love of design and art. Things took off from there. I ended up running a web agency with my then business partner, who used to be signed to Mo’ Wax Records, recording under the name RPM. For some reason he decided we made a good team, and it turns out we did. I’d do my coding, and he’d do his music and bring the aesthetic to the mix. We started our little business at the start of the dot com boom and rode that wave. Well, I say we got lots of money, it wasn’t lots of money; we were making enough to pay our rent in London, and there was a certain amount of VC cash that came our way to make beautiful things. But then the dot com bubble burst, and it was like, oh no, what do we do now? My business partner just wasn’t feeling it, and went off to Bristol to become a yoga teacher, as you do. I was left holding the small-business baby. From there I realised I wanted to help nonprofits, and started to grow the agency by building good-looking, usable websites for good causes. Over the next 10 years I got to the point where I had five or six staff and a quarter of a million pound turnover. And then I realised I was okay at managing a team of techies and designers to deliver things, but it wasn’t what got me out of bed in the morning. Clients liked the websites we made, but what they really raved about was my consultancy and my training. So I sold the web agency to a company called Engaging Networks and branched out on my own as a consultant. I started to think about how fundraising and campaigning could be combined, and became incredibly curious about what motivates people to give, especially online, where most of the stuff that makes people give is stripped away. That happened around 12, 13 years ago. I’m still whispering to donors, and I managed to write a book called [The Giving Code](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/156806749-the-giving-code).

**Kim**
I’ll back up a little, because there are a lot of threads here I’m finding really interesting. I previously ran a record distro, more of an anarchist punk thing. It’s interesting how many people I speak to through Geeks for Social Change have some background in music, DIY music, punk scenes, all the rest of it. I wonder if you could talk about what appealed to you about the design and music side, and where that came from.

**Rae**
I’ve been obsessed with music ever since I was a young kid. What I liked about the Peter Saville aesthetic was that it was quite anti-capitalist in a way. It was designed not to say “this is New Order”, for the most part. The design was there to stand out if you knew what you were looking for, and if you really loved the music, you’d know this was the thing you were there to buy. I really liked the humility of that. New Order were almost anti-pop stars, and I loved the ethos of Factory Records.

**Kim**
I live in Manchester, and I honestly find Factory Records a bit much. There are streets named after them everywhere. They’re weirdly revered as this local institution, and where I live it doesn’t feel like we’ve ever been allowed to move past it. Do you want to talk a bit more about what you liked about Factory?

**Rae**
They were about music for music’s sake, rather than trying to make money, as far as I could tell. The guy who set it up really wanted to develop artists. He was into the scene, and he wasn’t obsessed with profit; he just wanted to keep things going. Everything was part of a whole. You couldn’t separate the music from the design and the place and the buildings. It’s amazing how that led to this huge revitalisation of a post-industrial city. I like that they were all sticking two fingers up at the music industry. You had Stock Aitken Waterman churning out really formulaic pop.

**Kim**
That’s the contrast I didn’t really have at the time, because I didn’t see what it was a reaction to. It’s interesting hearing you say this, because now it feels like Factory Records has been a massive financial success, and that’s why the city council likes it. They’re probably trying to do more things in that image without really understanding what made it good.

**Rae**
I don’t see it as a massive financial success, because the Ha&#xE7;ienda always ran at a loss. New Order were more successful by accident than by design, and it was their royalties that basically kept the Ha&#xE7;ienda afloat. Without that goodwill from the band, the club, the whole scene wouldn’t have kept going. So from a capitalist point of view it was a complete failure. It’s weird that people celebrate it in capitalist terms, because actually it was successful despite the business, you know.

**Kim**
They’ve kind of cashed in on it nowadays. Not so long ago I noticed that the average advertising hoarding here has these naff graphics with Morrissey and New Order and Oasis on them. You think of all these bands that were almost more famous for their backdrop. It’s the same with Black Sabbath in Birmingham: those bands were good because there was nothing else to do.

**Rae**
The music scene in Manchester is so exciting. I’ve really been getting into Another Country recently, and I feel that same ethos coming through in what they do. Their aesthetic, their video art, their live performance are all part of a whole; you can’t separate it out. It’s very anti-establishment, very underground. You can still get into an Another Country gig for a tenner, and there’s a really magical atmosphere at their gigs.

**Kim**
I’ll have to check it out. I’ve lived here far too long and got too cynical, but I’ll go check them out. I was going to ask you about the transition you described, from web stuff to direct giving, and working out what it is you care about, which is something I really empathise with. Geeks for Social Change is about technology too, but I realised that technology is just a useful thing people need, and I’m indifferent to that part. The part I care about is doing it with other people to make a change in the world. The tech is just a thing people need, and the skills come quite easily to me. There’s a theme here about the overall packaging that comes from music, and I wonder if there’s a way we apply that to our causes, where everything has to work together. But the question I have next is: what are the causes you care most about? And did that come into it when you were designing this Donor Whisperer idea?

**Rae**
The causes I care most about tackle wealth inequality and environmental degradation, and support people who are most marginalised. I’m not into causes that treat the symptoms; I’m into causes that go right to the root of the issue, which is very frustrating, because progress is scant and it takes years to see any fruit. The things I’ve achieved that I’m most proud of, like helping loan-shark companies like BrightHouse and Wonga go under. Debt Hacker was founded by an eccentric Scottish multimillionaire who wanted to see the end of payday loan companies. He hired a woman called Lily Kaprani, who formed a team, and we had the mission to take down the payday loan companies. We knew we needed to do two things. One, spread the word among people who’d built up ridiculous sums of debt just from borrowing a few hundred quid, not being able to make the repayments, and then being charged 50% interest or something. We needed to tell those people they could claim all of that money back. And we were standing on the shoulders of other campaigners who’d done the groundwork and made that possible.

**Kim**
So why did that happen? Was it a legal change?

**Rae**
It was, yeah. If you’re lending money, you have to take into account someone’s ability to repay, and the payday loan companies weren’t doing that. So we could reclaim millions. We just had to tell people it was possible, then direct them to the software this guy had built, which let them make a claim really easily. We also built up a list of people on the campaigning side who’d help spread the word. It wasn’t long before Wonga went bust, and then BrightHouse.

**Kim**
Congratulations.

**Rae**
Thank you. It was one of the most exciting things I’ve ever been involved in. And then there was Debt Resistance UK. I was paid to do the Debt Hacker stuff, but the debt resistance work was a labour of love, something voluntary I did when I lived in Newham. I was contacted by a campaigner called Joel Benjamin, who’s involved with Move Your Money and was campaigning against the big banks. He’d spotted that a lot of councils had got themselves into really toxic relationships with banks, with these products called lender option borrower option loans. Newham Council was the worst case; they’d borrowed billions using these dreadful products. We worked out that about 70% of our council tax was going on interest payments to banks from these things. We realised we could use the law brought in by Eric Pickles, I think, where citizens could audit their council’s finances. So we used that to request the actual loan documents from all these big banks. And when I was doing that, I spotted that the loans from Barclays were pegged to LIBOR, and I realised Barclays had been done for fixing LIBOR. I pointed that out to the auditor.

**Kim**
What’s LIBOR again, sorry?

**Rae**
LIBOR is the rate at which banks lend to each other. The auditor, from Ernst & Young or one of the big accounting firms, was there, and I pointed it out to him, and his face went pale. He said, “oh, yeah.” After that, that firm of auditors resigned.

**Kim**
Wow. Why did his face go pale, to be clear?

**Rae**
I think he realised the game was up. The audit firms’ real customers are the big banks; the councils are just pocket money for them, something they do as a civic duty.

**Kim**
They did the audit presumably assuming it would be private, and now it’s not anymore. Got you.

**Rae**
So the auditors changed, and they wrote me a letter saying that if I spoke publicly about what I’d discovered, I could be arrested. And I was like, screw that. So I carried on.

**Kim**
And were you arrested? No. Funny, that.

**Rae**
I think the popo have more important things to do, like going after people wearing Plasticine Action T-shirts. But anyway, the end result was that Newham Council took Barclays to court and got back 50 million pounds for Newham. I talk about it like I did it all myself; I really didn’t. I was part of a team of really incredible people.

**Kim**
I think this is a really interesting example of what we were just talking about, because in my own attempts at systemic change, a lot of it is just really, really boring. What’s more boring than LIBOR rates, interest rates between banks, legal changes, making little bots to interpret them, paperwork, FOI changes? It all sounds boring. And even in that, you’ve done a thing that required this whole little constellation of things to happen. And afterwards, is it even repeatable? When I look at the charity-sector work that does sustain itself, the charities that go on for decades, I don’t know how many of those things are true. If I think about the impact statements we get asked to do, is making Wonga go bust something you can put in your impact statement? It feels like a completely different mentality. And dare I say it, your laser focus on the goal. Maybe I’m getting too old and trying to call the things I do punk to make myself sound cool. But there’s something cool about that, just laser focus on: we’re going to change this one thing, we’re going to make it happen, and we’re going to get all these people together to do it.

**Rae**
In a sense it’s a drop in the ocean for Barclays; they’re not going to go under because we did that.

**Kim**
But a lot of people got their money back, right?

**Rae**
Yeah, that’s true.

**Kim**
How many people? Do you have an exciting number for that?

**Rae**
I don’t, actually. I’m not sure what the total was in the end. It was maybe four million pounds we got back for people.

**Kim**
And probably more importantly, the company went bust and couldn’t do it anymore. That’s pretty good.

**Rae**
And it’s really nice. I feel quite privileged to have had a thing where I could see the result, because so many people working on systemic change don’t get that.

**Kim**
I think those experiences change you, too. Are there any other big campaigns that were as impactful for you personally?

**Rae**
Well, there was one, but things didn’t turn out how I hoped.

**Kim**
This sounds exciting. Or interesting, at least.

**Rae**
I was part of a campaign called 64 for Suu, back in the mid-noughties, when things felt more changeable than they are now. It was funded by the Foreign Office at the time. The idea was to get people all around the world to send 64-word messages to celebrate Aung San Suu Kyi’s 64th birthday, because that’s eight times eight, which is a lucky number in Burma. It really took off, and we got great press coverage. And then the best thing of all happened: the site got hacked. We’d had to build the site over a bank holiday weekend, and the brief was to make it really secure, because we had intelligence that the Chinese government were going to pay hackers to take it down. Negotiating with hosting companies to put security measures in over the bank holiday weekend was not much fun. But it went live when it was supposed to, and it all worked. Then it got taken down, and that was actually perfect, because we could go back to the press and say, look, this site’s been hacked, and we got it back online within hours. That meant a new wave of interest. It was one of a long chain of events that led to the release of Aung San Suu Kyi, which I now have very mixed feelings about, given what’s happened since. But at the time it felt really huge.

**Kim**
It’s hard doing things internationally like that, isn’t it? Everyone’s trying to do their best, but you never really know what the situation is.

**Rae**
The Burma campaign kept telling us, can you try not to make it all about Aung San Suu Kyi, because there are loads of political prisoners who’ve done just as much, if not more, for the movement. And the thing is, you can’t. If we’d focused on them, we wouldn’t have got the publicity. Now I see why they were saying that. It’s a constant battle in my mind: do you go for the thing you know will get maximum exposure, or the thing you know will be more beneficial in the long run but will be a huge struggle to even pay for itself?

**Kim**
What’s the battle that’s winnable for you, and what’s the battle that’s winnable for someone else? The liberation of the Burmese people is, on one level, for the people who live in Burma. You saw an opportunity for something winnable. Not everything is winnable. You saw an opportunity to take something with a sense of virality and do something with it. And sometimes it says more about us, too. I’ve found that a lot in the work I’ve done. Sometimes you have your own little act of resistance so you can feel like a human being, and that can be as important as the thing you’re doing itself, which might be someone else’s battle. I find this stuff really complicated and fascinating. Take, for example, the [“Not In Our Name” (NION) letter](https://notinourname.org.uk/), which I’ve got a lot of respect for. This is the one with a lot of, I think, mostly cis women allies, basically saying “fuck you” to all this transphobia. It’s funny, because on one level it doesn’t help me very much as a trans woman. On another level, I think it’s great that so many people are being encouraged to express themselves like that and put their foot down, in a way that makes me think that if they’re in a workplace or a public place, they’ve already emotionally prepared to be an ally in that setting. And again, it’s something that was winnable for them, something relatively easy to put their name to. I think they maybe have plans to donate to trans causes. But everything goes down its own little rabbit hole as soon as it starts and takes on a life of its own. I’m a big fan of that line, the little poem about how it’s going to take all of us coming at it from all directions to make a change. I love that. So I’m really fascinated by this segue from web work and campaign work into your practice now, which I think people would like to hear about, the Donor Whisperer. Was this planned? Did you just fall into it? Tell us a little about your current iteration.

**Rae**
I completely fell into it, or that’s what it felt like. For my 30th birthday, my family compiled this big book of 30 things they liked about me. It was very sweet. One of them was a little essay I’d written when I was 10 about what I’d be doing in 20 years’ time. For some reason I’d written that I’d be writing software programs to raise money for charity. Given that was back in the 80s, it’s almost a perfect description of what I was actually doing. So maybe I didn’t fall into it at all. Maybe this was always what I wanted to do; I just didn’t put two and two together. Maybe it comes from the pressure I put on myself to do things that are worthwhile. I found doing campaigning websites, it was always really difficult to justify from a sales point of view.

**Kim**
I really empathise with that. This is why I started doing my own thing, too. So many people have three to five grand, they want a website, and they think it’ll fix everything. People do need websites, don’t get me wrong. But they also need, and I think you’ve taught me a lot of this, sales and marketing strategy. The website’s only half your problem. The other half is how you get anyone to look at it.

**Rae**
Exactly, 100%. I realised it was going to be a lot easier to make a case for my work if I could say, this is how much you were raising before, then we did this thing, and now look how much you’re raising. So that naturally got me into fundraising. It also let me put a value on campaigning, because running a campaign brings in loads of people who then give money, but who are also encouraged by the campaign being won and become loyal to the cause, because they’ve seen how things change when they take action. That meant I could genuinely put a financial value on the work I was doing. It led me to think: what is it that prompts a human being to give money for not really getting anything back? It seems like such a weird thing to do from a survival point of view. Why would you give away your resources like that? I was very inspired by the work of a guy called&#xA0;[Chris Rose](https://www.campaignstrategy.org/), who literally wrote the book on how to win campaigns.

**Kim**
What’s the book called?

**Rae**
How to Win Campaigns.

**Kim**
Oh, good title.

**Rae**
Yeah. And later on, What Makes People Tick. He taught me so much about values, and how they’re linked to human behaviour. What you actually value drives a lot of your decision-making, and most people just aren’t conscious of it. That led me to apply the theory of basic human values to fundraising, and it’s been extraordinary how well it’s worked.

**Kim**
Perhaps for people who don’t know, do you have a one-paragraph spiel on what the Donor Whisperer is?

**Rae**
I help small to medium-sized charities get more donors and raise more money from their existing list.

**Kim**
I was very cold to this to begin with, and it took me a while to come round to it. Some of it’s ego, some of it’s pride, but some of it is just that, coming from a trans anarchist feminist perspective, we don’t really have any culture of doing our own fundraising. In this country there’s a big sense of, someone should write a funding bid, and then no one does it. I’d previously been quite successful at those, but I find it leads you down a weird alley where everything becomes about the funder and not about the people you’re working with. So I’m kind of answering the question for you here. A lot of people think that when you talk about direct giving or donors, you’re talking about cold calling, or doing things they don’t want to do, which I’ve now learned isn’t true. But do you want to talk a bit more about your approach and what it feels like?

**Rae**
There are so many rants I could go on here. One of these days I’m going to have a big rant about accountants and how they’re messing up the world, and another about funders and how they’re messing up the world. I really, really hate chuggers. I hate door-to-door fundraisers. And I hate poverty-porn ads and guilt-trip fundraising, and I think everyone does. Who enjoys being accosted in the street by some bouncy guy with a clipboard? It’s done so much damage to good causes, people associating fundraising with being made to feel guilty, or feeling social pressure. Good fundraising is not like that at all. It should be a moment of real joy, where you’re showing someone how to achieve something they long for deep down, and helping them make it happen. It’s like being, and this is going to sound so cheesy, a doula for good stuff. For change. For societal change. Now, I’m talking about this in a way that’s aligned with my own values, but I’m very aware I have a specific set of values. When I give money to good causes, I want that money to make a real change in society, something big and visible and systemic. A lot of causes aren’t set up to do that, and that’s okay, because there are lots of people whose values differ.

**Kim**
Doing trans activism for so long, it took me a lot to understand that there’d be people outside my immediate group who’d want to donate without being involved, or who have different politics to me. Coming from the feminist DIY squat scene, the setup is normally: you squat a building, you put a festival on, or maybe you get some funding to pay for a building, you put on bands and workshops, and then it ends, and if you break even you’re happy. So my question is, when you think about direct giving, are there certain things that are friendly to fundraise for? Are some things in and some out? I know from talking to you, for example, that animal rights people do very well with it. Are there any characteristics of what makes a good direct-giving campaign?

**Rae**
It all depends what values your very best donors have. Animal rights charities and children’s charities get so many donations because their cause fits perfectly with the values of people I call hobbits.

**Kim**
This is a good time to talk about this, because it’s quite fundamental. Do you want to tell us about your values map? Do the little talk.

**Rae**
My work is based on a marriage of the work of Chris Rose and Shalom Schwartz, who came up with the universal theory of human values. You may be familiar with a&#xA0;[map of human values](https://commoncausefoundation.org/_resources/the-values-map/)&#xA0;that has 10 or 12 segments. There are words on the map like universalism, benevolence, respect for elders, accepting my portion in life, adventure, esteem of others. All of those values cluster together in different ways. What I realised was that you could divide the map into three. As a shorthand to help people understand values, and how they drive people to do really weird things that I don’t understand, I gave each of the three portions of the map a name relating to the work of J.R.R. Tolkien. So one part of the map is people with what I call hobbit values, another is dwarvish values, and another is elvish values. Tolkien’s work is really useful, because it maps onto the different value groupings so perfectly. People I’d describe as hobbits are driven by comfort, safety, familiarity, tradition. All the hobbits just want to stay in the Shire, have a nice cosy life, eat lots of food, and associate with people like them. Then you’ve got the dwarves, who are driven by being seen to be the hero. They like the finer things in life, luxury, and they’re all about derring-do and being seen by others to do amazing things. And then you’ve got the elves, who are focused on the bigger picture: universal harmony, restoring the land to its former good state, battling evil, beauty, new ideas and change. I realise not everyone’s a fan of Tolkien, so you can also map this quite nicely onto the Star Trek universe: think of Bajorans as similar to hobbits, Ferengi as similar to dwarves, and humans as similar to elves. That’s another way, if that’s a universe you feel more comfortable in.

**Kim**
I love this, and I use it all the time. I’d maybe describe myself as a recovering elf. It was really interesting, because in almost all the contexts I’ve been in, I’m an elf in the company of elves. We all have to be right, and it’s all a bit high and mighty. Don’t get me wrong, everyone’s incredibly dedicated and willing to go to extreme personal danger to get stuff done. But we’re all a bit insufferable, too, and this helped me realise that. I especially see this split on social media. I spend a lot of time on Mastodon, and people there tend to be very elfy. They want a future, they want things to be different, and they can be quite blind to the fact that there’s more than one way to get there, or that they’re leaving people behind by going that far. When I think of dwarves, I think of some of the communities we don’t spend much time with, but that I really respect, who are trying to take things like cryptocurrency and decentralised autonomous organisations and think about money, or startups. There are a lot of people trying to get poor people to make AI startups they can make a living from, and it’s completely anathema to the Mastodon people. They just don’t see eye to eye. And then, to be honest, I’m increasingly becoming a hobbit. But a lot of the hobbit thing for me is about personal comfort: I’m into privacy, hosting things myself, open source software. It can be quite individualistic. But there’s also something in that, the people insisting on privacy, where for them it’s a bit prefigurative and it does make it better for everyone. The thing I’ve learned from working with you so often is that when we talk about causes, we’re literally speaking a different language. For example, one of the values on the map that gobsmacked me the first time I saw it was national security as a personal value. But look at what happened in Scotland with trans rights: it was literally a national security issue that the government couldn’t agree on trans issues, and it caused the downfall of the government. No one’s making the campaigning argument that placing these insane boundaries on bodies is destabilising democracy, because everything else then has to revolve around it, and we’re letting the worst actors in the world into that gap, actors we know have identified it as a strategic place to intervene. It’s literally affecting our national security. I find it really fascinating to take almost anything you believe and look at it from any other perspective. And for anyone listening on the podcast, we’ll put the map in the article so you can look.

**Rae**
Yes, that’s genius, that’s so good. People get the idea that with a certain cause you can only ever fundraise from elves, or only from hobbits. But actually, for any given cause, and I’m going to lay down a challenge for myself here, you give me any cause and I’ll show you a way to fundraise for it from elves, hobbits and dwarves.

**Kim**
And the hard thing is you have to step outside yourself to do it. This is where I found a lot of the ego in the stuff I was doing, and still am. It’s intimidating to think beyond you and your friends and your immediate network. It takes a lot of courage to say, here’s what I stand for, and then try to reach strangers, who hopefully then aren’t strangers, they become your friends, because you’ve found a way to connect.

**Rae**
For sure. Nothing prepared me for this work better than going door-knocking for a political party. I know that sounds weird, but especially with the algorithms and social media the way they are, we get so easily corralled into tiny groups of people who are exactly like us, and that makes us even more like the people we hang out with. People are being pushed so far apart from one another. There’s nothing like going from one door to the next in a neighbourhood to jolt you back into the reality of what other people think, and how differently they think from you, and to actually formulate an argument to say, look, you need to vote differently. That’s kind of my, and this is going to sound really cheesy, my dojo.

**Kim**
A lot of people who’ve done door-knocking tell me this. To be honest, they can be a bit smug about it, too. “Oh, on the doorstep.” Yeah, okay, you go out every night and talk to people on the doorstep, you don’t have to be so smug about it. Yes, I’m sure your perspective is better than mine.

**Rae**
I’ve since realised I actually injured myself really badly, because I overdid it with the door-knocking, and my mobility is much more limited now. I physically can’t go door to door. So the way I jolt myself out of my bubble is by reading comments on social media, which is a difficult but necessary part of my job.

**Kim**
I find that hard nowadays too, just because of how much of the internet is bots. I increasingly distrust anything I read online. Bots plus AI feel like the majority of what I see out there.

**Rae**
LinkedIn is one of those places that’s full of dwarves, and it’s not really my place. But the advantage of LinkedIn is that it’s very difficult to be a bot there. Yes, there’s more and more AI slop, but because everyone has a verifiable identity, even if someone’s using AI, or large language models, that real human being has prompted the model to come out with whatever it came out with.

**Kim**
They’re at least putting their name to it and saying, I believe this thing. God, it’s depressing how we’ve got to this place, isn’t it? I use Mastodon and LinkedIn, and maybe this is why: it’s the same on Mastodon, if you start posting LLM content you get crucified. But there’s also no motivation to, because there’s no advertising, no algorithm. It’s interesting, that’s why I quite like LinkedIn too. Everyone’s a bit on their best behaviour, and it’s cheesy and corny as fuck, but there’s a certain honesty to it. I don’t know.

**Rae**
The only issue is that LinkedIn is full of dwarves, so you’re just going to get the dwarvish point of view.

**Kim**
What makes a group become an attractive target for donations? Do people need to be incorporated to start? Do they need a nonprofit structure? What does it look like for a group to start thinking about direct fundraising?

**Rae**
I think thinking about structure is a big mistake at the start. A lot of people say, oh, I could go for charity status and reclaim Gift Aid, and it’s a lot of money. But the problem with charitable status is that it comes with so many "you can’t do this, you can’t do that"s, and you’re then beholden to the Charity Commission. That can be a huge problem, especially if you’re going after elvish donors, because the things the Charity Commission don’t want you to do are exactly the things people with elvish values do want you to do, like being politically active and speaking truth to power. The other problem with becoming a charity is that the UK has literally millions of them, and most raise less than a thousand pounds a year. If you genuinely think a charity structure is the way to go, find a way to sidle up to a bigger charity and say, can you create a fund for me to fundraise into? Yes, they’ll charge you a little for it, but then you don’t have to think about trustees, or audits, or accounts. There’s so much busy, stupid work you save yourself. My heart always sinks when someone says, I’m going to set up a new charity, because so often it’s about ego, not about practically getting the work done. So structure does matter, but it matters very little when it comes down to it for fundraising.

**Kim**
So then the flip side: what does make something attractive to give to?

**Rae**
What makes it attractive is trust. I have a bunch of mantras my clients get sick of hearing, and one of them is that people give to people. Especially now, when there’s so much distrust in society. If you’re a human being who’s willing to be open and transparent, willing to show your integrity, willing to say “this is who I am” with your full force of personality, and to tell stories about how you make change in the world or solve problems, and you’ve got all the receipts for it, that’s what makes a thing attractive to give to. The fact that you do what you say you will, and you can prove it. That’s really all there is to it.

**Kim**
So what kind of ways do you think people would go about proving it?

**Rae**
There are a number of ways. One is to tell a believable story about what’s happened, and then have real things you can point to: this is the person who got the benefit of this, or this is what this field looked like before and after. Having statistics, and monitoring and evaluating things, helps too, but that’s mainly if you want to fundraise from grant-making organisations; that’s their language. For most individuals, most donors just giving with their credit card, it’s the visual, audible or written storytelling, or video. Those are the things they want to see.

**Kim**
We went to Amsterdam for a conference, which was fascinating for me; I learned so much. More than anything, I was surprised that the default cultural expectation of the conference was, we’re all here to grow our email lists. It’s funny, because for all we talk about social media, and for all I’ve ranted about the queer scene’s reliance on Instagram, I feel like no one’s really thinking about email lists. If you were going to do one thing to get your start, to get your course going, is that what it would be, or something else?

**Rae**
Again, I’m going to give the stock irritating consultant reply, which is: it really depends on your audience, what channels are they using? The reason a lot of campaigners have got stuck on email is purely that it’s not owned by anyone. There’s no controlling authority over an email address, so you can take your email addresses with you if you change platform. Same with phone numbers and postal addresses. Whereas if you’ve got someone’s Instagram username, that isn’t going anywhere; you can’t own it, Meta controls it. That’s why there’s this focus on email, especially among digital campaigners. I think we’ll see a shift to phone numbers being the holy grail that fundraisers and campaigners want to collect, because the returns from email are getting harder. But for some causes it might be YouTube subscribers, for some email may still be the thing, for some it’s phone numbers. It really depends on the habits of their very best donors. Where are they hanging out? What channels are they using on a daily basis?

**Kim**
In the UK we’re in a context where political dissent of any kind is being massively cracked down on, and surveillance and securitisation feel like they’re rising. I’m hearing a lot recently about how hard it is to get a bank account for anything vaguely radical. We had a few problems setting up GFSC, and we actually had to go on Twitter and shame the bank into letting us have one. We’d asked around and found three other trans or person-of-colour causes this specific bank had denied an account for no reason. And talking of how divided people are, we don’t even have one social network to moan at each other on anymore, do we? I also hear about sex workers being deplatformed for doing non-sex-work things when people find out they’re a sex worker. So you’ve kind of answered this already, and I know we started talking about digital sovereignty, but do you have any feelings on this landscape, on what mechanisms to use, where you put your money, that kind of thing?

**Rae**
It’s so tough, because the most effective way for charities to grow their lists at the moment is basically via Meta. And it’s horrible. I hate it. All my clients hate it.

**Kim**
So specifically Facebook adverts?

**Rae**
Sometimes Instagram, depending again on the audience they’re going after. I feel nauseous every time I set up an ad campaign, because I know where that money’s going. One thing I’d desperately love to make happen is to find a way of breaking out of that ecosystem, to connect with people who have the values, motivations and habits that align with a specific cause. Some marketing agencies have taken steps towards this, like Mosaic, where you give them a postcode and they can tell you the people there are most likely to have this lifestyle or whatever. But it doesn’t tell you about their values. What we really need as a community is some kind of ID marker, whether that’s an email address or a phone number, and then a way of establishing what values and motivations the associated person has. At the moment it’s frighteningly easy and cheap to do that on Meta, because you can run tests that show people different messages associated with different values, and see which they go for.

**Kim**
So in a way it’s weird, because you get the entire enterprise toolset that the biggest companies on Earth have, but you can access it as an individual. Is that what makes it different?

**Rae**
Oh, it’s extraordinarily powerful. It’s like being a scientist with the whole world as your lab.

**Kim**
The first time I looked at it, it gave me a real insight into the degree to which I was being targeted.

**Rae**
It’s one of the biggest ethical dilemmas I struggle with, that the way I know best to get new donors for charities is by using this advertising system, taking the learnings, and applying them to other channels. If only Bluesky had a similarly sophisticated and effective advertising system, I’d be there like a shot. It’s a real source of pain to me.

**Kim**
Something we’ve talked a lot about is “no ethical consumption under capitalism”, which took me a long time to actively absorb. Maybe it’s an elfish value to believe you can somehow be immune to it, until you realise it’s just consumer choice all the way down. Do you have any advice on fundraising and banking for people who are likely to be deplatformed? I even saw the Canary got debanked the other day. Did you see that story?

**Rae**
Yeah, I’m not happy with the Canary at the moment.

**Kim**
I’m not sure exactly what happened, but even the idea that your bank can just turn around and say, you know what.

**Rae**
It happened to Debt Hacker. Mailchimp just switched us off. There was no appeal, because they thought we were trying to exploit people who were in debt. And fair enough, I would shut those people down too. But there was no human coming in, looking at the stuff, and going, okay, maybe you guys are doing something good here.

**Kim**
They assumed the worst of everyone. I found the same trying to do business stuff. The base assumption of every law we have is that you’re always doing the worst thing for the worst reasons.

**Rae**
How to destroy the banking system, that’s part of my expertise. How to work within it is not. There’s a little voice in my head going, crypto, crypto, and I’m like, shut up. No, no, no.

**Kim**
That’s why I keep saying to the crypto people, everything they do is just so complicated. I don’t think it solves any of my problems yet, but I’d love it to one day, which is why I’ve always got one side-eye on it. We took an international donation and it took about six months to come through, and we had to get a fiscal host to avoid paying tax on it. It was just crazy. So I’d like some solution to this, but fundamentally it’s got to interact with cash very easily, and it’s complicated. Talking of threats to direct giving, I feel like the cost of living crisis is really affecting people’s ability to give. What do you think this looks like for the future of the sector? It’s hard not to feel like the UK is now in permanent economic freefall, and maybe even for what we’re doing, we need to start looking overseas for income, though it could be the same everywhere. Do you have a feeling for what the rest of the decade looks like for people doing this kind of work?

**Rae**
We’re actually about to have an enormous windfall in charitable giving. This is very counterintuitive for a lot of people, but if you think about it, the richest generation that ever lived are about to start, well, they have started, passing on, and they’re leaving legacies.

**Kim**
This is boomers, right?

**Rae**
Exactly. There’s going to be a huge transfer of wealth, and it’s an urgent imperative to make sure that wealth goes to good causes rather than just the grandkids. After that’s happened, it’s anyone’s guess what happens to the landscape of giving. As someone who technically falls into Generation X, though I’m more of a millennial at heart, it’ll be interesting to start focusing on that generation and how they give. I suspect giving to institutions will completely evaporate, and people will give on much more of a Patreon basis, direct to single individuals doing interesting stuff.

**Kim**
Bodes well for us, at least.

**Rae**
It does. The question for you guys is how you target the wealthy boomers. Your cause could be really exciting and attractive to people with dwarvish values, and potentially, like you were saying about the privacy thing, there’s maybe a bunch of hobbits who’d be into it. It’s going to be so much fun for you to experiment and see what makes the dwarvish boomers and the hobbit boomers open their wallets.

**Kim**
And just for you, do you have a big political goal at the moment? What’s your current cause?

**Rae**
I really like what the&#xA0;[Good Law Project](https://goodlawproject.org/)&#xA0;are doing; I think they’re at the vanguard of the resistance to everything that’s bad in the UK. I’m also spending a lot of time and money supporting the&#xA0;[Green Party](https://greenparty.org.uk/). I have mixed feelings, and it’s a continual struggle as to whether it’s the right thing, but I can see how, if Green policies were brought in in the UK, it would be a genuinely joyful place to live.

**Kim**
I’m also a reluctant Green Party supporter. And member. So if people want to know more about you, get involved, they like what you’re saying and want some of it, tell us what you’ve got to offer. We know you have a course and a book.

**Rae**
Yup. So go to my website, [donorwhisperer.co.uk](http://donorwhisperer.co.uk/) and buy a copy of my book if you’re into that. If you’re into that you can get a Kindle/PDF version or if you like the smell of new books you can get a printed copy. If you are a small-to-medium-size charity or social enterprise or good cause and you’d like to find out how to get a lot of new donors very quickly, then please book an hour of my time. You can get that for free, and I’ll spend it working with you to put together a plan of how you can achieve that, and I will give you an honest view on whether I’m the right person to help you, or whether someone else might be better. I’m not sure how long I’m going to keep that going for, but it’s there at the moment.

Many thanks, Rae, for that interview. This blog post is also available as part of the  Geeks for Social Change podcast.
